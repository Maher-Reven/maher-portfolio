## Context

BOTS ran an automated investment platform, and was preparing to expand into the US and Asia. The initiative arrived scoped as a single sentence: **add payment integrations for the new markets.**

I led it across Mobile, Backend, Compliance, Legal and Product.

## The problem was not payments

Breaking the requirements down with Legal and Compliance, it became clear that payments were only the visible part.

The existing KYC implementation had been built around a single region. Document types, identity handling and address parsing were hard-coded — while each new market required materially different identity artifacts. The US wanted an SSN; the Asian markets wanted their own local documents entirely.

> The headline was payments. The underlying problem was identity.

The roadmap concealed a dependency chain that ran the other way from how the work had been scoped:

```txt
payment integration
 └─ KYC / identity requirements
     └─ legal eligibility
         └─ ability to launch
```

Shipping a payment surface without resolving KYC first wouldn't have been a late feature. It would have been a regulatory exposure. So rather than let engineering start against assumptions, I ran a working session with Legal to define the regional compliance states **before any code was written**, and reframed the initiative from a payment integration into a compliance-first market entry.

## The state problem underneath it

There was a second, quieter issue. KYC verification is asynchronous, and the architecture didn't respect that.

A provider could mark a user approved while our system still had them pending. Webhooks arrived late or not at all, retries failed silently, and a user could reach checkout while their internal compliance state hadn't caught up. It was a distributed-state problem with no single source of truth — and the thing it put at risk was a regulatory boundary.

The fix was to invert the order and make compliance a gate rather than a check:

```txt
before  user → payment → KYC
after   user → KYC → cleared → payment
```

The payment module simply would not initialise until the orchestrator emitted a cleared state.

## Region-aware by architecture, not by branching

Rather than encoding each country's rules into the mobile app, we put an event-driven **KYC orchestrator** between the app and the provider, and split the three concerns cleanly.

The app collects information, presents region-specific requirements, and handles interruption and recovery. The orchestrator decides what a user needs based on geography, owns the verification state, absorbs asynchronous provider events, retries and webhooks, stays auditable, and enforces the gate. The provider does identity, document and liveness checks.

The decision that mattered:

> The provider performs verification; we own the state.

That kept one consistent internal model while leaving the provider and the regional rules free to change underneath it. The state machine made `Pending → Cleared → EDD` explicit, with payments locked until the right state was reached.

## Shipping it without over-building it

A fully automated global compliance engine was months of work and would have missed the market window. Rushing it would have created legal exposure. So the rollout was phased.

Phase one was the minimum architecture needed to enter the markets safely: dynamic orchestration, US and Asian identity requirements, region-aware document requests, a hard payment gate, reliable webhook and state handling, end-to-end validation. Complex automated edge cases were deferred on purpose.

Phase two would be decided by production telemetry once the funnel was actually observable, rather than by imagined problems. Ship a safe foundation, measure reality, then optimise.

## Treating failure as the normal case

Mobile verification does not happen in a clean environment. Apps get killed, networks vanish, users switch away mid-flow, documents come out blurry, provider responses land whenever they land. We treated all of that as ordinary operating states rather than edge cases.

Verification state lived in the orchestrator, not in the app's memory, so a user returning after an interruption resumed at the right point. State-driven UI behind feature flags meant the US and Asian flows could be switched on without an app-store release. And sensitive information was requested progressively — the SSN only at the point it was legally required, not up front.

Payments sat behind all of it. We validated across providers in sandbox against insufficient funds, expired cards, network and provider failures, then ran controlled real-money verification to prove the settlement path end to end. Every transaction was idempotent and retried, so flaky mobile connectivity couldn't produce a duplicate charge. The rule was enforced by the architecture rather than by the interface: payment was programmatically unreachable until KYC had succeeded.

## Leading it across six functions

Mobile, Backend, Product, Compliance, Legal and Design all had a stake, and my job was to be the join between them — keeping engineers pointed at execution while I handled the dependencies outside their control.

Stand-ups stayed on blockers. Leadership got progress translated into business risk, dependencies, mitigation and launch readiness. RFCs and architecture reviews put Mobile and Backend on the same compliance-state model, and I worked between Product, Design and Legal on the real trade-off: legal requirement against user experience against delivery speed.

The point was never more meetings. It was making sure one function's decision didn't silently invalidate another team's work.

## Results

```txt
markets live    2 → 4
onboarding      +25%
payment volume  +35%
support load    −30%
```

The payment volume lift in the quarter after the US and Asia launch unlocked further global revenue. But the outcome I'd point at is architectural: KYC stopped being a single-region onboarding feature and became a reusable compliance layer with seams cut for the next provider, market and rule.

The business had asked *"how do we add payments in Asia?"* The more useful question, which took a working session with Legal to surface, was *"what has to be true for us to legally and safely operate payments in Asia?"* That one distinction changed the scope, the stakeholder map, the architecture, the delivery sequence, the risk model, and the definition of done.

## What I'd do differently

<Retro>
I'd have gone looking for the dependency at roadmap time instead of finding it during breakdown. It was discoverable earlier — the single-region assumptions were already in the code — and finding it a stage sooner would have cost the organisation nothing, where finding it a stage later would have cost a launch.

I'd also have treated observability as a deliverable rather than a by-product. The old onboarding flow was a black box: users could fail verification without anyone being able to say where or why. We only got the telemetry to answer that as a consequence of rebuilding the funnel, and it turned out to be the thing Product and Support wanted most. That was worth scoping deliberately, not inheriting by accident.
</Retro>
